# Money basics: saving before investing

Investing gets the attention, but it sits near the end of a sequence. The steps
below are the conventional ordering in personal finance — not personal advice,
and not the right order for everyone. Circumstances differ, and a licensed
advisor can weigh yours.

## Spend less than you earn

Every other step depends on this one. There is no return high enough to
outrun a persistent deficit, and no budgeting method that matters more than the
gap between income and spending. Tracking for a month is usually enough to find
where the money actually goes, which is rarely where people assume.

## Build a starter emergency fund

A small buffer — often cited as $1,000, or one month of expenses — is what
keeps an unexpected car repair from becoming credit card debt. It belongs in a
high-yield savings account (see [[hysa-vs-checking]]) where the balance cannot
fall.

## Capture any employer match

If an employer matches 401(k) contributions, contributing enough to get the
full match is an immediate, guaranteed return on that money. Very little else
in finance offers that. This is why the match is usually taken before paying
down anything but the most expensive debt.

## Clear high-interest debt

Credit card interest often runs above 20% APY. Paying that down is a guaranteed
return equal to the interest rate — higher than the long-run average return of
the stock market, with no risk and no waiting. Low-interest debt (many
mortgages, some student loans) is a different question, since money invested
instead might reasonably earn more.

## Finish the emergency fund

Three to six months of expenses is the common target. More if income is
variable or a job is hard to replace; less if it is stable and well-insured.
This is what makes a job loss survivable without selling investments at the
worst possible moment.

## Then invest, with a horizon

Money you will need within a few years does not belong in the stock market —
the range of one-year outcomes is far too wide. Money you will not touch for
decades has historically done well there, and the Backtest tab lets you see
both the growth and the drawdowns that came with it.

## Habits that matter more than picking well

**Automate it.** Money moved on payday, before you see it, gets saved. Money
saved from what is left at month end usually does not.

**Start early.** Compounding rewards time far more than it rewards size.
Contributions in your twenties do work that much larger contributions later
cannot replicate.

**Keep costs low.** A 1% annual fee sounds trivial and consumes a large share
of a lifetime portfolio. Index funds exist largely because of this arithmetic.

**Do less.** The most common expensive mistake is selling during a crash and
buying back after the recovery. A boring plan you actually stick with beats a
clever one you abandon.

## What this site is and isn't

This tool is educational. It can show you what allocations did historically and
explain what the numbers mean, but it does not know your income, debts, taxes,
family, or timeline. For decisions that depend on those, a licensed financial
advisor or tax professional is the right call.
