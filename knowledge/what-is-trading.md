# What trading actually is, and its pros and cons

**Investing** is buying an asset to hold it while the underlying business or
economy grows. **Trading** is buying and selling to profit from price movements
over shorter periods. Same instruments, different source of return, different
skills, and very different odds.

## Where the profit comes from

An index-fund investor earns money because the companies in the index earn
money. Everyone holding can profit at once — the pie grows. Over the long run
the market has trended up, so time is working for you.

A short-term trader earns money because they bought from or sold to someone else
at a better price. That gain came out of another participant's pocket. Before
costs, trading is close to a zero-sum game between participants; after
commissions, spreads, and taxes, it is negative-sum in aggregate. Traders as a
group must underperform the market by the amount of their costs, because they
collectively *are* the market.

That does not mean nobody profits. It means profit requires being better than
the person on the other side of the trade — and that person is often a firm with
faster data, lower costs, and a team of researchers.

## Common styles

- **Scalping / day trading** — many trades within a day, holding minutes to
  hours, nothing held overnight.
- **Swing trading** — holding days to weeks to capture a move.
- **Position trading** — holding weeks to months; shades into investing.
- **Algorithmic / high-frequency** — automated, sometimes microseconds.

The shorter the horizon, the more the outcome depends on execution, costs, and
luck rather than on anything about the underlying business.

## Two ways people decide what to trade

**Technical analysis** studies price and volume — charts, trends, support and
resistance, indicators. It assumes patterns in price carry information.

**Fundamental analysis** studies the business — earnings, debt, margins,
competition — and asks whether the price is wrong relative to value.

Long-horizon investing leans fundamental. Short-horizon trading leans technical,
and the evidence that chart patterns reliably predict short-term returns after
costs is weak and contested.

## The case for trading

- **Speed.** Gains do not require decades.
- **Two directions.** Traders can profit when prices fall, via short selling or
  options, while a long-only holder cannot.
- **No idle capital.** Positions can be closed and redeployed.
- **Skill has some room.** In specific niches, disciplined participants with a
  genuine edge do make money consistently.
- **It is engaging**, and it teaches how markets actually clear.

## The case against

- **Most people lose.** Regulator-mandated disclosures from retail brokers
  routinely show that a majority of retail accounts lose money on leveraged
  products, and academic studies of day traders find only a small minority
  profitable after costs, with fewer still persistently so.
- **Costs compound.** Spreads, commissions, and slippage are charged on every
  round trip.
- **Taxes punish it.** Short-term gains are taxed at ordinary income rates,
  materially higher than long-term rates. See the taxes article.
- **It takes real time.** Active trading is a job, and it competes with
  full-time professionals.
- **Behaviour works against you.** Overconfidence, chasing losses, cutting
  winners early and holding losers late are well-documented and expensive.
- **Leverage is everywhere in trading**, and it is what converts a bad stretch
  into a wipeout. See the leverage article.

## Trading is not the same as gambling — but it can be

The distinction is whether you have an **edge**: a reason to expect positive
returns on average. A casino game has a fixed negative expected value; no amount
of skill overcomes it. Trading has no fixed house edge, so a genuine edge is
*possible*.

Without one, though, trading is functionally gambling with extra steps and worse
entertainment value — random entries, costs on every trade, and a negative
expected outcome after fees. The market does not care which of the two you are
doing, and the P&L of a lucky beginner looks identical to that of a skilled
trader over short samples. That is the trap: short-term results are dominated by
noise, so it takes a long time to learn whether you had an edge at all.

## What the evidence supports

For most people, most of the time, a low-cost diversified portfolio held for a
long time has beaten their own attempts at trading it. The reasons are
unglamorous: lower costs, lower taxes, fewer decisions, and no requirement to be
right about timing.

If trading appeals anyway, the conventional risk-management advice is to treat
it as a separate, small, clearly-bounded pot of money you can afford to lose
entirely — kept apart from long-term savings, and without leverage while
learning.
