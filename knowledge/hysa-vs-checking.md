# HYSA vs. traditional bank accounts

A high-yield savings account (HYSA) is an ordinary savings account that pays a
competitive interest rate. The money is just as safe and just as reachable as
money at a big-name bank — the difference is what the bank pays you for holding it.

## The gap is larger than people expect

Big traditional banks often pay very little on savings — frequently well under
0.10% APY. Online banks and credit unions competing for deposits have paid
several percent when short-term rates are high. On $10,000, the difference
between 0.01% and 4% is roughly $1 a year versus $400 a year, for identical
risk and identical access.

That gap is not a reward for taking more risk. It is mostly a reward for
switching, because large banks with branch networks and captive customers do
not need to compete on rate.

## Checking vs. savings vs. HYSA

**Checking** is for spending — debit card, bill pay, direct deposit. It
generally pays nothing, and that is fine, because the balance should be small.

**Savings** is for money you might need soon. Traditional bank savings and a
HYSA do the same job; the HYSA simply pays more.

**A HYSA is a savings account**, not a separate product category. Transfers to
your checking account typically take one to three business days, which is a
mild feature: it adds just enough friction to discourage impulse spending.

## Is the money safe?

Deposits at FDIC-insured banks (and NCUA-insured credit unions) are federally
insured up to the standard limit per depositor, per bank, per ownership
category. An online bank with FDIC insurance carries the same government
backing as a bank with branches on every corner. Always confirm the institution
is insured before depositing.

## What an HYSA is for — and what it isn't

An HYSA is the right home for an **emergency fund** (commonly three to six
months of expenses) and for money earmarked within the next few years: a car,
a house down payment, a wedding. Its value is that the balance does not fall.

It is the wrong home for a decades-away retirement. HYSA rates roughly track
inflation over long periods, so purchasing power grows slowly at best. That is
the tradeoff: stocks have historically grown far more over decades, but can
fall 50% along the way — unacceptable for money you need next year, and
survivable for money you need in thirty.

## The rate is variable

HYSA rates float with short-term interest rates set by the Federal Reserve. A
4% account can become a 2% account without notice. This is not a bait and
switch; it is how savings rates work. Compare rates occasionally rather than
assuming today's number is permanent.
