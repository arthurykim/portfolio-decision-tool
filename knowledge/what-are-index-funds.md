# What are index funds?

An index fund is a fund with no stock-picker. Instead of paying a manager to guess
which companies will win, it mechanically holds everything in a published index — a
rules-based list like the S&P 500 (the ~500 largest US companies) or the Nasdaq 100.
The fund's only job is to match the list.

## Passive vs. active investing

An active fund employs analysts trying to beat the market; an index fund just *is* the
market. Decades of data show most active funds underperform their index after fees over
long periods — largely because they charge roughly ten times more while collectively
being the market they're trying to beat. That arithmetic is why index investing became
the default advice for long-horizon savers.

## Index fund vs. ETF — which word means what?

The two terms overlap but describe different things. "Index" describes the *strategy*
(track a list). "ETF" describes the *wrapper* (trades on an exchange). An index fund
can be an ETF (VOO) or a traditional mutual fund (VFIAX) — same S&P 500 inside, just a
different way to buy it. Most of what this site tracks are index ETFs: both things at
once.

## Why the index matters more than the fund

Funds tracking the same index are near-interchangeable — SPY and VOO both track the
S&P 500 and move nearly identically; VOO simply charges a bit less. The choice that
actually shapes your outcome is *which index*: a total-market index (VTI), a
large-cap index (SPY/VOO), a tech-heavy index (QQQ), or an international index
(VXUS) behave very differently, as the Backtest tab makes easy to see.

## The trade-off

An index fund guarantees you'll never beat the market — you own the average, minus a
tiny fee. Historically that "average" compounded to roughly 7–10% per year for broad US
stock indexes over long horizons, with brutal interim drawdowns along the way. The
Markets and Backtest tabs on this site let you explore both halves of that sentence.
