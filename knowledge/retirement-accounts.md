# Retirement accounts: 401(k), Traditional IRA, Roth IRA

Retirement accounts are tax-advantaged wrappers around your investments. The
investments inside (index funds, ETFs, bonds) can be identical — what differs is when
the government takes its cut. Contribution limits and income rules change most years,
so always check current IRS figures.

## 401(k) — the workplace account

A 401(k) is offered through an employer. Money goes in from your paycheck before
income tax, lowering this year's taxable income; it grows untaxed; withdrawals in
retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Two features make it powerful: contribution
limits far higher than IRAs, and the **employer match** — many companies add money when
you contribute (say, 50% of your first 6%). A match is an instant, guaranteed return;
capturing all of it is usually the first move in any savings plan. Many employers also
offer a Roth 401(k) option, which flips the tax treatment to work like the Roth IRA
below.

## Traditional IRA — pay tax later

An IRA (individual retirement account) is one you open yourself at any brokerage. The
traditional version works like a 401(k): contributions may be tax-deductible now,
growth is tax-deferred, withdrawals in retirement are taxed as income. It fits people
who expect a *lower* tax rate in retirement than today. Early withdrawals before
retirement age generally face income tax plus a penalty.

## Roth IRA — pay tax now

The Roth flips the order: you contribute money you've already paid tax on, and then
growth and qualified retirement withdrawals are completely tax-free. Decades of
compounding that will never be taxed makes the Roth especially attractive for young
savers currently in low tax brackets. Roths also have no required withdrawals in old
age, and direct contributions phase out above certain income levels.

## Traditional vs. Roth in one line

Traditional bets your tax rate will be lower in retirement; Roth bets it's lower right
now. Uncertain? Splitting between both hedges the bet.

## The common thread

All three are *accounts*, not investments. An empty IRA earns nothing — after opening
one you still choose what to buy inside it, which is where diversified index funds
(see "What are index funds?") usually enter the story. This site is educational and
doesn't give personal advice; a licensed advisor or tax professional can apply these
rules to your situation.
