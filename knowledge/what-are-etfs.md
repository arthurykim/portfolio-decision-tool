# What are ETFs?

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a basket of investments — stocks, bonds, or other
assets — that trades on a stock exchange like a single stock. When you buy one share
of an ETF, you're buying a tiny slice of everything inside the basket.

## Why ETFs exist

Buying hundreds of individual stocks yourself would be expensive and slow. An ETF does
the bundling for you: one purchase spreads your money across the whole basket. SPY, the
oldest large US ETF, holds all ~500 companies in the S&P 500, so one share gives you a
sliver of Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, and the rest at once.

## How they trade

ETFs are priced continuously during market hours, and you buy or sell them through any
brokerage exactly like a stock. That's the main difference from a traditional mutual
fund, which only trades once a day after markets close.

## What they cost

ETFs charge an expense ratio — a yearly percentage of your money skimmed to run the
fund. Broad index ETFs are extremely cheap (often 0.03%–0.10% per year, or $3–$10 per
$10,000 invested). Specialized or actively managed ETFs charge more, sometimes ten
times more, which compounds into a real drag over decades.

## The kinds you'll see in this tool

Every ticker on the Markets tab is an ETF: stock ETFs (SPY, VOO, VTI, QQQ, VXUS),
bond ETFs (AGG, IEF, TLT, BIL), a real-estate ETF (VNQ), and a gold ETF (GLD). They
differ only in what's inside the basket.

## What ETFs don't do

An ETF doesn't remove market risk — a stock ETF falls when stocks fall. It removes
single-company risk: one bad earnings report can crater one stock, but it barely moves
a 500-company basket. Diversification narrows the range of outcomes; it doesn't
guarantee good ones.
