# Taxes on gains, and why holding longer is treated better

In a taxable brokerage account, the tax you owe depends on two things: whether
you **sold**, and if you did, **how long you held** the investment first. Both
rules push in the same direction — toward holding.

*US federal rules, described for education only. Rates change, states add their
own tax, and individual situations vary. Confirm anything specific with a tax
professional or the IRS.*

## Unrealised gains are not taxed

If you buy a fund at $10,000 and it is worth $15,000, you have a $5,000
**unrealised gain** — a gain on paper. You owe nothing on it. You can hold for
thirty years while it grows and never pay a cent of capital gains tax along the
way.

The moment you sell, the gain becomes **realised**, and it becomes taxable in
that year. Nothing about the investment changed; only the tax bill did.

This single rule is why "buy and hold" has a mechanical advantage that has
nothing to do with predicting markets. A holder controls *when* the tax event
happens. A frequent trader triggers one on every profitable sale.

## Short-term versus long-term is a cliff, not a slope

How long you held decides which rate applies:

- **Short-term** — held one year or less. Taxed as **ordinary income**, the same
  brackets as your salary.
- **Long-term** — held **more than one year**. Taxed at preferential rates,
  which for most people is 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income.

The difference is large. Someone in a 32% income bracket pays 32% on a
short-term gain and 15% on the same gain held one day past the year mark. On a
$20,000 gain that is $6,400 versus $3,000 — about $3,400 for waiting.

The one-year line is a cliff. At 364 days you pay the high rate; at 366 days you
pay the low one. The holding period starts the day *after* you buy and includes
the day you sell.

There is also a 3.8% net investment income tax on investment income above
certain thresholds, which stacks on top for higher earners.

## Tax drag: the cost of realising early

Paying tax early hurts twice — you lose the money, and you lose everything that
money would have earned afterward. Compare $10,000 growing at 8% for 30 years:

| approach | ending value (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Hold the whole time, sell at the end | ~$86,000 before tax, ~$74,000 after 15% |
| Sell and rebuy every year, 15% tax each time | ~$62,000 |
| Sell and rebuy every year, 32% short-term rate | ~$45,000 |

Same investment, same 8% return, same 30 years. The difference is entirely how
often gains were realised and at what rate. This gap is called **tax drag**, and
frequent trading maximises it.

## Losses offset gains

Realised losses subtract from realised gains. If you have $8,000 of gains and
$3,000 of losses in a year, you are taxed on $5,000 net. If losses exceed gains,
you can deduct up to $3,000 against ordinary income per year and carry the rest
forward indefinitely. Deliberately realising losses to offset gains is called
**tax-loss harvesting**.

The **wash-sale rule** limits it: if you buy the same or a "substantially
identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is
disallowed for now and added to the cost basis of the new position.

## Cost basis and which shares you sell

Your **cost basis** is what you paid. Gain is sale price minus basis. If you
bought the same fund at several prices, the shares you choose to sell change the
gain — selling your highest-basis shares first realises the smallest gain.
Brokers default to first-in-first-out, which sells your oldest and usually
lowest-basis shares, so this is worth setting deliberately.

Inherited investments generally receive a **stepped-up basis** to the value at
the date of death, which can erase unrealised gains entirely for heirs.

## Dividends are taxed even if you never sell

Dividends are taxable in the year received, whether or not you reinvest them.
**Qualified** dividends get the long-term rates; **ordinary** (non-qualified)
dividends are taxed as income. Bond interest and most HYSA interest are taxed as
ordinary income. So a taxable account produces some tax every year regardless of
whether you trade.

Funds can also distribute **capital gains** to shareholders when the fund itself
sells holdings. Broad index funds and ETFs tend to distribute little, because
they trade rarely — one reason they are considered tax-efficient. Actively
managed funds often distribute more.

## Where the account type comes in

None of this applies inside a 401(k) or IRA. Those accounts are tax-deferred or
tax-free, so selling inside them triggers no capital gains tax at all. That is
why rebalancing and any higher-turnover strategy are usually better placed in a
retirement account, and why broad, rarely-traded funds fit well in a taxable one.

## The honest summary

Holding is favoured by the tax code in three separate ways: unrealised gains are
untaxed, gains held over a year are taxed at lower rates, and deferring tax lets
the untaxed amount keep compounding. None of that means an investment should be
held when there is a good reason to sell — taxes are one input, not the decision.
It does mean that a strategy of frequent buying and selling has to clear a real,
quantifiable hurdle before it beats simply doing nothing.
